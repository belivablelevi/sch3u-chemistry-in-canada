Chemistry in Canada

Chemist One

Peter Winterburn

1962 – 2019 · Exploration geochemist · Professor, University of British Columbia

Peter Winterburn was a geochemist — he used chemistry to find metal deposits buried too deep to see. Trained in England, he spent decades at mining companies in Africa and South America, rising to Chief Geochemist at two of the world's largest mining companies.

In 2013 he became a professor at UBC, leading exploration research across more than sixty countries. He died in 2019, killed in an attempted robbery on a family trip to Chile.

Sources →
His Main Contribution

Finding what you can't see

Most ore deposits are buried under metres of rock and soil. You can't drill everywhere hoping to get lucky — Winterburn's work was about finding a smarter way to look.

The idea is called pathfinder geochemistry: a buried deposit slowly leaks trace elements upward through soil and groundwater. By sampling soil and testing for these elements, geochemists map where they cluster — and that map points to what's hiding below. Winterburn's UBC team used exactly this in Chile's Atacama Desert, tracing salty surface patches back to a buried copper deposit.

What Is Geochemistry?

Chemistry, applied to the Earth itself

Geochemistry combines chemistry and geology — it studies which elements and minerals are in rocks, soil, and water, and how they move and react over time.

Why His Work Matters

Smarter searching, fewer holes in the ground

Before modern exploration geochemistry, mining companies found deposits mostly by drilling and hoping — slow, expensive, and disruptive. Winterburn's techniques narrow a huge search area down to a specific target before a single drill goes into the ground.

Interesting Facts
01
His PhD research was on kimberlite — the same rare volcanic rock that carries natural diamonds up from deep inside the Earth.
02
He wasn't searching for gold or copper directly — he was reading chemical "leftovers" in ordinary dirt.
03
His fieldwork and research took him to more than sixty countries, from South Africa to Chile to Canada's Northwest Territories.
04
In 2018, the year before he died, he helped organize a major international geochemistry conference held in Vancouver.

SCH3U Connection

  • Elements & the periodic table — pathfinder elements are elements straight off the periodic table.
  • Ionic compounds — minerals like copper carbonate hydroxide are held together by ionic bonds.
  • Solubility — some compounds dissolve more easily than others, which is why certain elements travel upward through wet soil.
  • Identifying substances — geochemists identify elements by their unique chemical properties.
Chemist Two

Orlando Aloysius Battista

1917 – 1995 · Polymer & cellulose chemist · McGill University

Orlando Battista was born in 1917 in Cornwall, Ontario, one of eight children in a poor family. He studied chemistry at McGill University, then spent his career working on polymers — long, chain-like molecules — eventually specializing in cellulose.

A devout Catholic and prolific writer, Battista published more than a dozen popular-science books aimed at everyday readers, not just chemists.

Sources →
His Main Contribution

Cutting cellulose down to its strongest core

In the 1950s, Battista turned ordinary cellulose fibres into something new: microcrystalline cellulose (MCC). Treating fibres with controlled acid hydrolysis and sound waves stripped away the weaker parts, leaving only the strongest, most tightly-packed core.

He named it Avicel, and it became one of the most widely used ingredients in pharmaceuticals — a free-flowing powder still found in millions of pills today.

What Is Cellulose?

The molecule that holds plants up

Cellulose is a natural material in plant cell walls — it gives plants their structure, the way a skeleton holds up an animal. It's a chain of thousands of glucose molecules joined end to end.

01

The plant

Cellulose starts in every plant — cotton is one of the purest natural sources, sometimes over 90% cellulose.

02

The fibres

Zoomed in, cotton is made of thin, twisted fibres — themselves bundles of even smaller cellulose strands.

03

The cell wall

Inside a plant, cellulose is arranged into layered cell walls — a natural structural material, similar to how concrete is reinforced with steel bars.

04

Long chains, held together twice

Each cellulose molecule is a polymer: glucose units joined by covalent bonds into a chain. Chains then stick to each other with hydrogen bonds — weak alone, strong in huge numbers.

05

Strong structure

Cellulose is strong for something made of sugar because millions of bonds act together, not just one.

06

Microcrystalline cellulose

Battista's process kept only the most tightly-bonded, crystal-like sections. The result, MCC, flows so well it's used to bind powdered medicine into tablets like these.

Why His Work Matters

An ingredient hiding in plain sight

MCC became one of medicine's most important "invisible" ingredients — an excipient that binds powdered medicine into a solid, stable tablet. It's also used as a low-calorie bulking ingredient in some foods. Almost everyone who's taken a pill has used Battista's chemistry without knowing it.

Interesting Facts
01
He grew up poor in Cornwall, Ontario, working as a child to save enough money for his first typewriter.
02
He didn't invent a new material from scratch — he used acid and sound waves to strip an existing one, cellulose, down to its strongest possible form.
03
He held roughly 58 U.S. patents and hundreds more internationally — nearly one for every year of his working life.
04
In 1985, the American Chemical Society gave him the Anselme Payen Award, the top honour in cellulose chemistry.
05
He wrote more than a dozen popular-science books, because he believed regular people — not just scientists — deserved to understand chemistry.

SCH3U Connection

  • Covalent bonding — glucose units in one chain are joined by covalent bonds, formed when atoms share electrons.
  • Intermolecular forces — separate chains are held together by hydrogen bonds.
  • Polymers — cellulose is glucose repeated thousands of times into one long chain.
Chemistry Careers

Two Canadian careers, built on this chemistry

Both careers grew directly out of the work above — one studies Earth's chemistry, the other works hands-on with materials in a lab.

01

Exploration Geoscientist (P.Geo.)

What is the job? Studying rock, soil, and water to find where mineral deposits might be hiding — Peter Winterburn's actual career.

Where does chemistry come in? Geoscientists test soil and rock for elevated "pathfinder" elements, using how compounds form, dissolve, and move through groundwater to read the results.

Chemistry concepts: the periodic table, ionic compounds, solubility, identifying substances.

Work environment: fieldwork in deserts, forests, or the Arctic, plus lab and office analysis.

Why chemistry matters: it turns ordinary-looking dirt into a specific drill target — without it, exploration is guesswork.

Marcus Dall Col, via Wikimedia Commons (CC0). Source →

  • Grades 11–12

    High school

    SCH3U/SCH4U Chemistry and Earth & Space Science are typically required or recommended.

  • University

    Earth science degree

    A four-year Bachelor's degree in geology, geochemistry, or a related earth science, from an accredited Canadian program.

  • Specialization

    Geoscientist-in-Training + experience

    Graduates register as a Geoscientist-in-Training (GIT) and complete four years of supervised experience.

  • Career

    P.Geo. registration

    After a practice exam, geoscientists earn the P.Geo. designation, required to practise independently in almost every province.

02

Chemical Technologist

What is the job? Preparing, testing, and analyzing materials in a lab — the hands-on work behind materials like Battista's MCC.

Where does chemistry come in? Running reactions, checking purity, and confirming a material has the right properties before it's used in a product.

Chemistry concepts: chemical properties, purity and identification, polymers, safe handling of reactions.

Work environment: a lab, often inside a pharmaceutical, food-science, or industrial chemistry company.

Why chemistry matters: technologists run the tests that prove a material is safe and ready to use — chemistry is the whole job.

TLSuda, via Wikimedia Commons (CC0). Source →

  • Grades 11–12

    High school

    SCH3U/SCH4U Chemistry and SPH3U/SPH4U Physics are typically required for admission to college chemical technology programs.

  • College

    Chemical laboratory technician/technology diploma

    A two-to-four-year diploma (e.g., Chemical Laboratory Technician) covering chemistry, lab safety, and analytical techniques.

  • Certification

    OACETT certification

    Graduates can apply for certification with OACETT (Ontario Association of Certified Engineering Technicians and Technologists), earning the C.Tech. designation.

  • Career

    Working technologist

    Employment in a pharmaceutical, food-science, or industrial chemistry lab, with room to advance.

Why Canadian chemistry matters

Winterburn and Battista worked in completely different fields, but both show the same thing: chemistry is rarely about big, dramatic discoveries — it's about noticing small details everyone else overlooks. Canada's mining, pharmaceutical, and materials industries still depend on scientists doing exactly that kind of careful work today.
